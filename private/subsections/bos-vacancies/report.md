Filling Board of Selectmen Vacancies in Andover
===============================================

*What the town charter and the state statutes require when seats on the Board of
Selectmen fall vacant, and why a further resignation would not force an
election.*



Executive summary
-----------------

Two of the five seats on the Andover Board of Selectmen are vacant. Both were
last held by Republicans. The three remaining members are Democrats.

That arrangement has prompted a natural question: if Anne Cremé also resigned,
would the board lose its quorum, be unable to meet, and therefore be forced to
an election, with Anne free to run again?

The first half of that intuition is correct. A two-member board could not
conduct ordinary business, because the charter fixes the quorum at three
regardless of how many seats are filled. The second half does not follow.
Filling a selectman vacancy is a separate power that survives the loss of a
quorum. Under state law the remaining members fill the seats, and if they do
not, the town's Republican elective officers do. Neither path is an election.

The only route that puts a seat before the voters is a citizen petition, the
same mechanism that produced the selection of Carol Lee on April 11, 2025. A
further resignation would not trigger that. It would remove the third Democratic
vote that today must consent to any appointment, and hand the choice to Jeff
Maguire and Scott Person, or failing them to a pool of Republican officeholders.
The lever that actually reaches the ballot is the petition, not resignation.



The situation
-------------

The charter sets the size and party balance of the board.
[Section 401](https://charter2024.andoverct.info/#Section401) provides:

> There shall be a Board of Selectmen consisting of five (5) members. [...] No
> more than three (3) members of such Board shall be members of the same
> political party.

Three seats are filled, all by Democrats. Two seats, both last held by
Republicans, are vacant. The question examined here is whether a third
resignation, reducing the board to two members, would force those seats to an
election. It would not, and the reasons are worth setting out in full, because
the conclusion runs against the intuition.



What a quorum requires here
---------------------------

The charter uses the strict form of the quorum rule.
[Section 403](https://charter2024.andoverct.info/#Section403) provides:

> The majority of the entire Board of Selectmen shall constitute a quorum, and
> no ordinance, resolution, or action except a vote to adjourn or fix the time
> and place of the next meeting shall be adopted by less than a majority of the
> entire Board of Selectmen.

"The entire Board" means all five authorized seats, not the seats currently
filled. The quorum is therefore three, and it stays three no matter how many
seats sit empty. A board of two could not adopt any ordinary measure. On this
point the intuition is right: two members cannot run the town's regular
business.

The mistake is to assume that this paralysis forces an election. It does not,
because filling the empty seats is governed by a different rule.



Filling a vacancy is a separate power
-------------------------------------

The charter's general vacancy provision,
[Section 208](https://charter2024.andoverct.info/#Section208), tells the
affected board to fill its own vacancies:

> Any vacancy, from whatever cause arising in any elective office, board,
> agency, commission or similar body of the Town shall be filled in accordance
> with this section within 60 days following the date of notice to the Town
> Clerk.

Its only backstop, in [Section 208B](https://charter2024.andoverct.info/#Section208B),
is that "in the event the affected board fails to act within 60 days, the Board
of Selectmen may then fill the vacancy." For a vacancy on the Board of Selectmen
itself, that backstop is circular. The affected board is the Board of Selectmen,
so the charter offers no separate body to step in when the board cannot act. The
charter alone leaves a gap.

State law fills the gap, and it is built precisely for a depleted board.
[Connecticut General Statutes Section 9-222](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_146.htm#sec_9-222) provides:

> When a vacancy occurs in the office of first selectman or in the office of
> selectman it shall be filled within thirty days after the day of its
> occurrence by the remaining members of the board of selectmen.

If the remaining members do not act, the statute does not call an election. It
routes the appointment to a defined pool of officeholders:

> If such a vacancy [...] is not so filled within thirty days after the day of
> its occurrence, the town clerk shall, within ten days thereafter, notify the
> elective town officers enrolled in the same political party as the first
> selectman or selectman [...] who vacated the office [...] and it shall be
> filled by such elective town officers within sixty days after its occurrence.

The whole architecture assumes the board may be short-handed. That is the point
of the phrase "the remaining members," and of the second-tier fallback to party
officers. Whether the statute fully displaces the charter's Section 208 for a
board that cannot reach a quorum is not perfectly settled, and worth a word to
town counsel, but 9-222 is the specific provision for selectman vacancies and
the special election Andover held in 2025 shows its machinery is live here. The
diagram below traces the sequence.

Why resignation does not force an election
------------------------------------------

Apply the sequence to the scenario in question. Suppose a third selectman
resigns, leaving two members. Those two are the "remaining members" the statute
names, and the vacancy-filling power is not conditioned on a full quorum, so
they can most likely appoint replacements. The one real doubt is whether two
members may validly appoint at all, given that Section 403 bars any action by
fewer than three. If they cannot, or if they simply do not act within thirty
days, the town clerk convenes the Republican elective town officers, who fill
the seats. In neither case is there an election.

There is exactly one path to a public vote, and it is a citizen petition. Under
Section 9-222 an appointee serves:

> until a special election called as hereinafter provided upon petition of a
> number of electors of such town equal to five per cent of the names on the
> last-completed registry list thereof, but not fewer than fifty such electors.

The petition must be filed within fifteen days of the appointment. If it is,
and if more than one candidate qualifies, a special election is held. If only
one candidate qualifies, [Section 9-224a](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_146.htm#sec_9-224a) 
provides that no election is held and that candidate is seated. That is exactly
what happened with [the Board of Selectmen vacancy of April 11,
2025](https://www.andoverconnecticut.org/registrar-voters/pages/general-voting-information):
a petition produced a special election, a single candidate qualified, and she
took the seat without a vote.

So the route to the ballot is the petition, and it is available after any
appointment, no matter who resigns. Resignation is not what opens it.



The vote a third resignation would give up
------------------------------------------

There is a further reason the resignation idea works against its own goal. It
gives up the one point of real leverage still available today.

With three members seated, the charter's quorum rule requires three affirmative
votes to fill a seat, because any action needs "a majority of the entire Board,"
which is three of five. With exactly three members present, that means all three
must agree. Anne's vote is pivotal right now. She can decline to supply the
third vote, and without it no appointee is seated.

Resigning trades that away. A two-member board no longer needs her consent,
because the appointment power passes to the two remaining members under the
"remaining members" clause, or, if they sit on their hands, to the Republican
officers under the fallback. The pivotal vote is worth more in the seat than out
of it.

A quieter option follows from the same rule. If the third member stays and
simply withholds consent, the board cannot reach three votes, the seats remain
unfilled past thirty days, and the fill passes to the Republican elective
officers, who may well produce the party-endorsed candidates the tradition
points to. Whether that is desirable depends on who those officers are, but it
is a lever that a resignation forecloses.



Who would choose if the selectmen do not act
--------------------------------------------

If the remaining selectmen fail to fill the seats within thirty days, the
statute hands the choice to the elective town officers enrolled in the same
party as the members who left. Both departed members were Republicans, so the
pool is the Republican-enrolled holders of Andover's elected offices. The
statute does not say how a person who holds two of these offices is counted. The
natural reading, and the ordinary rule for a deliberative body, is one member
with one vote, though I've found no statute or case that resolves this.

By that count there are twelve Republicans in the pool:

  Alan Roy, Liz Lokiec, Bill Desrosiers, Kim Person, Bree Lanzieri, Marcie
  Miner, Alicia Lee, Wally Barton, Shawn Covell, Leigh Ann Hutchinson, Jed
  Larson, and Kathleen Skorka.

Add Kevin Arnesen and Joan Ross if alternate members are counted as officers, a
point on which the statute is silent. A majority of whoever is properly in that
body carries each appointment, so a handful of votes would decide each seat. The
full roster, with every member's party and the shared-person deductions, is in
the companion file [`andover_9-222_pool.csv`](andover_9-222_pool.csv).

This pool is a set of appointers, not a slate put before voters, which is
another reason a resignation does not lead to an election.



The open seats cannot go to Democrats
-------------------------------------

One constraint holds across every path. The charter caps any one party at three
of five seats, and [Section 206](https://charter2024.andoverct.info/#Section206)
ties minority representation to [Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 9-167a](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_146.htm#sec_9-167a).
Three Democrats already sit on the board, so the Democratic cap is full. The two
open seats, whoever fills them, must go to non-Democrats.

The statute requires only that much. It does not require the replacements to be
Republicans; it forbids a fourth Democrat. Republican-by-custom is a tradition,
not a legal command. In practice that means the seats will be filled by
Republicans or unaffiliated appointees, and the tradition, not the statute,
supplies the preference for Republicans.



The lever that actually works
-----------------------------

If the goal is to put a Board of Selectmen seat in front of the voters, the tool
is the petition, and it is well within reach. Five percent of Andover's registry
list, roughly 2,400 active voters, is about 120 signatures. The petition must be
filed within fifteen days of an appointment. Gather the signatures, and the
appointee holds the seat only until a special election in which any qualified
elector, including a former member, may run.

That is the mechanism that seated a selectman in 2025, and it does not depend on
anyone resigning.
